₹22.3 crore out of a health-grant nodal account. The vendor portal still shows a login form

Between 12 and 30 October 2026, 46 unauthorised electronic debits moved ₹22,30,04,879 out of Tamil Nadu's Rural Development and Panchayat Raj department's Single Nodal Account at HDFC Bank's CIT Nagar branch. The money had been released by the Union and the state under the Fifteenth Finance Commission's Health Sector Grants for 2021-22 to 2025-26, meant for primary healthcare through rural local bodies. A Block Development Officer filed the complaint with the Chennai Police Commissioner. Seven arrests were reported on 14 November.

Update, 17 Nov: three of the seven accused have been remanded to judicial custody by the Saidapet Metropolitan Magistrate court; police put recovery so far at ₹39 lakh, about 0.17% of the stolen amount. Reporting now says the misused credentials belonged to staff at panchayat unions in Nagapattinam, Ramanathapuram and Tirupur districts — conflicting with an earlier report naming Thiruvallur, Ariyalur and Dindigul — and that some of the ~25 mule accounts sit in Karnataka, Kerala and Maharashtra. Investigators are examining the transaction portal itself, including the role of the technical team that built and maintains it. The portal remains unfixed: its API still answers 404 to every unauthenticated caller as of 17 November.

We could not test the heist itself, the evidence sits with the police and the bank. So we did the next best thing: we read the portal the money moves through, from the outside, the same way anyone on the internet can. This page separates what the police complaint asserts, what we verified on our own probes, and what remains genuinely unknown.

TL;DR, the three systems
  • The SNA (Single Nodal Account) is how most central scheme money now reaches states: one account per department per bank, credited by the Union ministry, disbursed onward by state officers. SPARSH is the newer version of the same idea. The account belongs to the Director of Rural Development and Panchayat Raj; operational authority is delegated to Block Development Officers.
  • The portal is the web application BDOs log into to raise a payment — pick a beneficiary or contractor, enter amount, submit; a second officer (the "checker") approves. This is the maker-checker the complaint references.
  • The bank executes debits the portal's backend submits over its corporate banking channel (the bundle names NEFT payout paths and account/IFSC fields). HDFC Bank is the SNA's custodian here.

Our verification: the portal itself

On 15–16 November 2026 we examined the public portal, squareuplive.com/rdprd/ — with read-only probes from two vantage points (a US sandbox and a Mumbai VM), logged request-by-request in our case file NAR-2026-014. We held no account, submitted no credentials, and triggered no SMS or email. Findings F1–F5 below are our own case-file records; each was reproduced twice before we called it confirmed.

FindingWhat we observedStatus
F1, Login "encryption" is decoration The login page encrypts your username and password in the browser with AES-256-CBC using a fixed all-zero IV and two hardcoded passphrases embedded in the page's own JavaScript. The key that "encrypts" the password ships inside the page that asks for the password. Anyone with the page can regenerate the exact encrypted string. confirmed, bundle read, static
F2, Captcha is client-side The login captcha is generated and validated in the browser's own JavaScript; the server-side checks we could observe begin after sign-in. confirmed, bundle read
F3, OTP endpoints take identity + OTP in the URL Password-reset and sign-in OTP calls pass employeeId and the OTP itself as query parameters, the shape of a GET, not a protected POST body. We did not call these endpoints: doing so would fire a real SMS. confirmed, code path read; behaviour not tested
F4, Vendor and build fingerprints The app is a commercial off-the-shelf product: build namespace VMFTS_UI, an isSna=true build flag, NEFT payout vocabulary, beneficiary and fund-master screens, a footer credit to LeadWinner Corp, Hyderabad (leadwinner.com). The same vendor's SQUAREUP-branded FMS/GPMS funds-management suite is documented in public launch notes for other local bodies. confirmed, public web + bundle
F5, The API context answers 404 to everyone, from every network we tried Every anonymous request to the backend context (/rdprdbe, including the sign-in endpoint the app itself posts to) returns a bare Tomcat "resource not available" error, reproduced from the US and Mumbai vantage points and still true on 16 Nov. Either the backend is down, it is restricted to specific networks, or it was rebuilt and not redeployed publicly after the arrests. Until it answers, nobody can sign in through the public portal, including, presumably, the department's own officers. confirmed, 6 paths × 2 vantage points × 2 days

What this means for the incident: the complaint's theory of entry — misused maker-checker credentials of three specific BDO offices, is entirely plausible against this architecture, because the portal's protections stop at the browser edge. F1 means an intercepted or phished login submission is a stable, replayable string. F2 means the captcha never reached a server. F3 means the second factor travels in the most exposed part of a request. None of this proves how the accused actually got in, but it maps which doors were weak, and those doors are the ones the complaint names.

The vendor, and why RBI does not licence this product

A fair question after a heist like this: where was the regulator? The answer is that for this product, there isn't one. That absence is the finding.

LeadWinner Corp's SQUAREUP FMS/GPMS is a funds-management workflow application. It holds no customer money, issues no instruments, clears no transactions, and connects to no payment system of its own. Under RBI's regulatory architecture, every regulated function in this stack belongs to someone else:

  • The account is a bank account. HDFC Bank is the SNA's custodian; the account is governed by the bank–government relationship and MoF/RBI's SNA and SPARSH framework for central-scheme fund flows.
  • The payment is an NEFT debit the bank executes. NEFT is RBI-regulated — for HDFC Bank, the RBI-supervised member. The vendor never touches the rail.
  • The software is an IT service procured by a state department. No RBI licence class exists for "software that prepares payment instructions". The vendor is not a Payment Aggregator, not a Payment Gateway, not a White Label ATM operator, not a TSP to the banking system in the licensed sense.

The regulatory hook that does exist is outsourcing oversight: RBI's Master Direction on Managing Risks in Outsourcing (2023) makes a bank responsible for the security of service providers that handle its or its customers' information, and for monitoring critical outsourced systems. But that duty runs from the bank to RBI. Nothing in the public framework obliges the state department buying the portal to apply bank-grade vendor oversight, and there is no public registry of which banks' government business rides on which vendor's code. The vendor can ship an isSna=true build of a welfare-funds product to a state. No financial regulator has ever seen its architecture.

The ministry-side frameworks do specify security requirements, DFPA/SNA guidelines carry IT-security clauses, and CERT-In directions apply to everyone. But "the guidelines say so" and "someone tested it" are different claims. On the evidence available, nobody outside the vendor and the department had tested this portal before the money left.

Who pays: the heist drained health grants meant for rural primary healthcare. Replacement money, if any, comes from the same consolidated funds, the taxpayer. The mule-account trail and the arrests cost state police time. And every other state department running this vendor's software inherits the same unexamined architecture until someone audits it.

A related leak: the vendor's billing stack in a public repository

During the GitHub sweep for this case we located a public repository belonging to an apparent vendor-affiliated developer: a Spring Boot billing application ("Invoizo") with the vendor's support address in its configuration. The committed configuration file contains live-looking third-party service credentials, an SMS/WhatsApp provider account (SID and token), an email-service API key, a JWT signing secret, and a server address on a raw IP — all visible to anyone, committed in 2025, still public on 16 Nov 2026.

We have not used, validated or quoted any of these values; validating them would mean authenticating against third-party services on someone else's account, which our rules forbid outside an authorised scope. What matters at incident level: a vendor whose payment preparation software sat unexamined at a state department also ships credentials in public repositories. The disclosure pack for this is drafted and pending send.

What we did not find

Fairness on the record:

  • We found no evidence of a breach of HDFC Bank's systems. The bank appears, on the public record, as the account custodian whose channel executed the debits.
  • We found no evidence compromising the SNA/SPARSH architecture itself — the theft, per the complaint, happened inside one department's portal workflow.
  • We did not access the portal backend, hold any account, view any citizen data, or validate the leaked credentials described above.
  • We cannot confirm the reported "Nigerian gang" attribution or the "studied the portal from September" claim; those rest on police statements to press.

What would settle the open questions

  • The police: publish, in charge-sheet form, how credentials of the three named BDO offices were obtained, phishing, insider, intercepted traffic, or portal-side compromise. That single fact decides whether F1/F3 were load-bearing.
  • The department: say whether the portal's backend is intentionally offline, network-restricted, or broken, F5's 404s have now held for days across networks, and a welfare payments portal whose public sign-in is dead is itself a service failure worth a statement.
  • The vendor: disclose which other states and banks run this build, fix F1–F3, and tell every deployed customer whether their instance carries the same client-side crypto.
  • RBI and MoF: close the vendor-visibility gap, a registry of which software prepares payments against which bank's government accounts, and bank-grade security testing before a payments-adjacent product touches a nodal account.
  • An authorised test: our API-level questions (F3's server-side OTP validation, session handling, maker-checker enforcement) need a sanctioned engagement or the department's invitation. We will run it if invited.
