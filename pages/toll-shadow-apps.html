The FASTag toll scam — cash antagonism is not a cybersecurity strategy

India made cash the villain of tolling — and then shadow software on the digital rail itself printed clean-looking receipts for fraud, at ~100 plazas, for two years, while the national system recorded almost none of it. Police caught the scheme; the audit machinery never did. The lesson cuts against the reflex: the deficit was never cash, it was software nobody audits — and digitising further without fixing that just deepens the cyber risk.

TL;DR. Toll plazas had a cash side-door for vehicles without a valid FASTag: the driver pays double, the operator types the vehicle class by hand, a receipt prints, the agency is supposed to remit the money to NHAI. A small piece of software — referred to in court-adjacent reporting and the ED case as 'Mobdata' and 'Any' — let operators book only ~5% of that cash into the official system and pocket the rest, with plausible receipts hiding the theft. It was discovered because a citizen complained to Uttar Pradesh police in January 2025, not because any government system flagged it. A money-laundering case (ED, September 2026) is now the state's second attempt at accountability after courts quashed the first one.

1 · How a toll plaza actually works#

India's national highways collect toll electronically. Over 98% of vehicles pay via FASTag — an RFID sticker linked to a prepaid wallet, read by an antenna as the car passes. This is the NETC (National Electronic Toll Collection) system run by IHMCL, a NHAI subsidiary. The barrier lifts, the fee moves digitally. There is no cash and almost nothing for an operator to steal.

The other few percent — vehicles with no tag, a dead wallet, or a blacklisted tag — hit the manual side of the lane:

  • The barrier stays down. The booth operator keys in the vehicle class by hand.
  • Because the vehicle is non-compliant, the rules impose a 2× penalty toll, collected in cash.
  • A receipt prints from the plaza's official software (the Toll Management System, TMS).
  • The collection agency — a private contractor hired per highway package — batches the day's cash and remits it to NHAI on a daily/weekly schedule, keeping an agreed cut as its fee.

Two categories in the official software are keyed entirely by human judgment: exempted (ambulances, dignitaries, defence convoys…) and violation (tag-less vehicles). Nobody counts the cars independently. Remember that — it is the whole story.

2 · The scam#

In January 2025, UP STF raided the Atraila Shiv Gulam plaza on NH-135 near Mirzapur after a citizen complaint about cash being taken from tag-less vehicles without receipts. What they found was not a greedy booth clerk but a small software firm's work.

Alok Kumar Singh, 35, an MCA who had briefly worked at a toll plaza in 2021, had built a pair of applications — later referred to as 'Mobdata' and 'Any' — and rented them out to collection agencies across the country:

ElementWhat it did
Client app (booth PC)Ran on the plaza's own computers alongside the official TMS. Computed the same 2× fee from the public toll schedule. Printed identical thermal receipts on the same printer — drivers had no way to tell.
The 5% ruleOnly ~5% of non-FASTag transactions were keyed into the official TMS — just enough to make the day's exemption/violation numbers look plausible. The rest existed only in the shadow app.
Central monitoringPer-plaza collections streamed to a central portal and the operator's laptop. One person watched ~40 plazas' cash intake in real time.
The moneyCash stayed at the plaza; the developer's share moved through multiple bank accounts and wallets. STF estimated ~₹45,000/day from a single plaza.
The camouflage math. Non-FASTag traffic is ~2% of volume. Skimming 95% of that slice removes under 2% of gross revenue — invisible in any aggregate dashboard. A plaza remitting 98% of expected revenue raises no alarm. The fraud was designed to sit under the resolution of every report NHAI looked at.

3 · Why nobody caught it#

This is the part that should worry anyone who builds or audits digital public infrastructure:

  • Auditors audit the official system. The shadow app never wrote a byte to TMS. Every NHAI inspection of plaza software checked the one database the fraud carefully avoided.
  • There was no ground truth. No independent count of vehicles passing a lane (from barrier events, cameras or axle sensors) was ever reconciled against keyed transactions. NHAI announced AI audit cameras only after the scam surfaced — an admission of the missing control.
  • Plaza computers were operator territory. Collection agencies installed whatever software they liked on booth PCs. Remote-access tools on a toll computer are indistinguishable from legitimate vendor support.
  • Receipts were real. A driver who paid 2× cash got a genuine-looking receipt against a genuine-looking plaza. Consumer complaints would have caught the absence of receipts — not the wrong database behind them. (One Baghpat complaint in March 2026 — a receipt timestamped 8:46 PM for an 11:30 AM crossing — shows the signature persisting even after the crackdown.)

A national certification scheme for toll software exists — STQC's CTS mark, which requires exactly the properties that were missing (tamper-proof databases, audit trails, secure code review). There is no evidence it was ever a procurement requirement for plaza software. That question is now the subject of an RTI request.

5 · The names problem#

A note on 'Mobdata' and 'Any'. These two names come from ED-attributed wire reporting. We could not find either name in any app store, code repository, code-hosting site, or certificate-transparency log — which is consistent with internal codenames visible only on the seized devices and dashboards, not public products. Treat them as attributed labels, not established product names. (They are also unrelated to "MobData", a Chinese mobile-analytics SDK that happens to share a name.)

The named human actors: the developer (Alok Kumar Singh), plaza-level arrests (manager Rajeev Mishra, operator Manish Mishra, a fourth accused Saaban Lal Kumhavat), and two "system managers" — Sawant and Sukhantu — who ran day-to-day operations across the ~42-plaza network. The collection agencies' names are public; their culpability is precisely what the High Court said was not proven — and what the ED case is now trying to establish.

The DPI lesson: the scam ran on software, not on cash's existence. Deleting cash lanes deletes the one independent cross-check drivers and auditors had, while leaving lane software that no IT audit has ever inspected. Every new layer — unified plaza software, ANPR, digital notices — is more attack surface with the same near-zero audit capability. The next shadow app will not need a printer.

7 · Privacy & the state's stake#

Why the lane stayed dark: the state was buying data, not control. FASTag was designed as a data system, not a control system. Every NETC transaction clears through two centralised hubs (NPCI and IHMCL), building one of India's largest vehicle-movement databases with bank accounts attached. That data now prices the monetisation machine: toll securitisation, toll-operate-transfer (ToT) concessions and the NHAI InvIT all bid on FASTag-measured traffic. What nobody bought was per-transaction integrity at the lane — so a 10%-volume shadow app ran for two years. The state privatised the upside of collection; the shadow app was the operator privatising the leftover cash. This was argued back in December 2019 — before the mandate — in CashlessConsumer's analysis for The Wire, “FASTag: Will Datafication of India's Tolls Boost Highway Development?”, which flagged exactly this: centralised data without a data-protection law, anonymised instruments rejected in favour of traceability, blacklist geo-fencing potential, and the 4% ETC cut baked into every gazetted fee.

What the system knows about you. Every plaza pass generates: vehicle registration number, tag ID, issuing bank account, plaza ID, timestamp, lane, fee class — visible to your bank, NPCI, IHMCL and the agency. Non-FASTag vehicles surrender more data: the ANPR camera photographs your plate, and the receipt demands your details. CCTV images complete the set. The registry below even shows fee discrimination by home district — monthly passes are cheaper for vehicles registered in the district, meaning the system encodes where your vehicle lives. None of this had a data-protection law over it until DPDP 2023 — the mandate shipped in 2019, four years before the law. There are no published retention limits, no purpose limitation, and no public accounting of who queries the movement database. The Wire's parallel analysis — how economic transactions including FASTag were weaponised for mass surveillance after 26/11 — reads differently now that every post-scam fix (ANPR at every lane, MLFF plate-reading, bank-side VRN validation with blacklisting) deepens the same capture.

The scam is now the justification. Each fix is real — and each expands the dataset. None of the post-scam procurements we reviewed contains a consumer data-governance clause: retention limits, purpose limitation, access logs, or independent audit of who queries the movement database. The fraud is real, and so is the risk that it becomes the standing excuse for a surveillance infrastructure nobody chose.

8 · For the driver#

  • FASTag was never hacked. The RFID/deduction path is untouched by this case. Your tag and wallet are fine.
  • If you paid cash at a toll plaza (pre-Apr 2026), keep the receipt. If a receipt looks wrong — wrong time, missing plaza name, no TIN — that is now a known fraud signature; complain to NHAI's helpline (1033) with the receipt photo.
  • Today, a plaza that demands cash is itself non-compliant. UPI is allowed at 1.25× for tag-less vehicles; anything else, refuse and report.
