The Bank of Baroda breach, reconstructed from its own shadow

Between April and August 2026, the extortion group TripleX published a mirror of a Bank of Baroda SharePoint file estate — 162,111 files, 1,065.8 GB across 90 document libraries. No filesystem metadata survives in the mirror. This report reconstructs what the estate was, how it moved, and what the bank's own documents say about its controls — from filenames, folder grammar and byte arithmetic alone. Every figure traces to a numbered entry in a local evidence ledger; personal leaf data is excluded by design.

Chapter 1 — The incident

TripleX, a data-extortion outfit operating a leak-and-listing model, listed what it described as Bank of Baroda data on 12 May 2026 and later served a browsable mirror of a file estate from an onion service. The bank's securities filing of 27 July 2026 described a "potential business email compromise" affecting a "small number of customers". CERT-In and RBI involvement followed within days. The mirror went dark on 24 July 2026 and did not return during 30+ days of automated monitoring.

Chapter 3 — What was exposed

Content findings come from a 91-file high-signal download set, distilled without exposing personal data. Themes, in descending severity:

  • Identity and onboarding: a mule-account KYC ledger (9,504 rows, 32 MB) carrying 69,600+ ID-field instances (name, address, phone, email, PAN/Aadhaar-class fields); AEPS customer complaint bundles; digital-onboarding audit packs.
  • Credit book: an account-wise NPA ledger (March 2021, 713,742 rows ≈ ₹1.00 lakh crore) including 14,027 fraud-flagged accounts ≈ ₹31,987 crore — the bank's own credit-quality record.
  • Security operations: VAPT detail reports (KPMG observations, credential material), IS-audit packs for overseas territories (Fiji, Uganda, UAE, Botswana, Mauritius, Seychelles), a server-details workbook containing plaintext application credentials (counted, never reproduced), FIM/patching correspondence.
  • Governance: CBI FIR on alleged nepotistic transfers; compliance-forum minutes; policy corpus (KYC/AML 2018+2021, DR/BCP, compliance policy).
  • Inspection apparatus: zonal inspection packs naming 13 geographies; BoB World special-audit waves; concurrent-audit findings.
  • The NMC/Shetty record: nine documents naming the NMC/Shetty group — including a 31 Dec 2018 Oman annual audit listing the group's hospital entity as the #9 territory borrower on a stale rating, seventeen months before the red-flag dated 10 May 2020, and an inspection that began the day after Muddy Waters' December 2019 short report.

The pattern across all six: working paper — audits, registers, complaint bundles — not a core-banking extract. Core banking was, on this evidence, never the breached tier.

Chapter 4 — The metadata reconstruction (novel chapter)

The mirror exposes no filesystem timestamps, no ACLs, no ownership. It exposes names, paths, sizes, structure. This chapter is the methodology and findings of squeezing a bank's internal architecture out of those four properties — an L15 (depth-15) crawl, pseudonymized into a redacted inventory, loaded into a DuckDB bench, and interrogated in eight passes. Nothing below opens a single file.

4.0 Method and its limits first

Breadth-first enumeration to depth 15; convergence measured per level. L12→L15 contributed under 0.3% of the corpus (+4 files at L15 itself), so the census describes ≥99.9% of the mirror. Filenames parse to usable dates for only 17.8% of files — every temporal claim conditions on that subset, never extrapolates. A public inventory uses pseudonymized leaf tokens; counts survive, identities do not. Four things metadata can never prove: access, mtime, breach completeness, redaction fidelity. The full epistemics chapter is reproduced in the project repository (M8).

4.3 Naming-grammar forensics

Six pattern classes across 90 units: other 74.2%, numeric-id-name 17.7%, prefix-code 6.2%, regulatory-audit-term 1.7%, WhatsApp media 0.2%. Recurring gnrc_*_groupid and AuditInspectionZO* grammars mark centrally-provisioned templates instantiated per team and zone; personal workspaces break the schemes — the unmanaged-endpoint signature.

4.4 Temporal archaeology

On the 17.8% dateable subset, the cadence shows a living estate: inspection cycles, year-end closings (Dec 2023: 4,155; Dec 2025: 1,389) and a BoB World special-audit ridge (Apr 2025: 10,120 — the peak month). Units' last active months: department-sharepoint 2025-04, risk-compliance 2025-07, personal workspaces 2023-04. The estate was working paper, not a cold archive — and its freshness extends to within weeks of the leak window (Ch 5).

4.5 Duplication topology

10,656 duplicate clusters (normalized name + exact size) cover 28,182 files; redundant bytes beyond first copy: 100.2 GB (9.4%). The largest redundant artefacts are security tooling — a Tenable Core ISO (10.75 GB) stored twice, a 4.2 GB pcap twice, Nessus feed archives thrice. Zero files sit in folders named like backup directories: redundancy here is manual copy-as-backup, not enterprise backup. Every duplicate is one more location that had to be secured, and one more copy an intruder could take.

4.6 Sensitivity surface (care tier)

Eight filename-cue regex classes (Aadhaar, PAN, passport, voter-ID, ration card, driving licence, KYC-set, photo/signature), aggregated with a k≥5 floor: 559 files (0.3%) carry at least one ID-document cue — largest classes PAN (189) and KYC-set (175). Concentration pattern matters more than counts: KYC evidence is smeared across audit packs, zonal returns and personal workspaces. That is why a folder-tree leak becomes an identity-leak problem even when no identity database was touched.

4.8 Post-series probes

Three follow-on probes sharpen the picture. (1) Dual estate: byte-vs-count divergence and UNC-style archive naming show at least two storage systems feeding one mirror — a bulk file server plus SharePoint libraries. (2) Group-site census: the gnrc_<team>_groupid grammar yields dozens of per-team group sites with uniform templates — one grant on the parent covers them all. (3) The daily-series clock: an MSME Digi-Udyam daily-report series (548 files, 513 filename-dated) stops at 24 June 2026 — five days after TripleX's listing — giving the collection floor of Ch 5.

Chapter 5 — The temporal floor

Filename clocks beat press timelines. The latest dated artefacts: an mConnect Android build stamped 25 June 2026, and the Digi-Udyam daily series ending 24 June 2026. If that series kept updating on the live system after 24 June — unobservable from outside — the collection/exfiltration window collapses to ~24–25 June 2026 ± days. Either way, one property stands: collection continued for roughly six weeks after the group's 12 May listing itself — consistent with TripleX's add-and-repost pattern on other listings, and inconsistent with a single-day theft narrative.

Chapter 6 — Attribution: the insider question

A source narrative holds that the breach originated with a disgruntled employee who posted a SharePoint link in retaliation for a transfer. Tested against the corpus, five structural findings bear on it:

  1. Breadth: 90 roots across departments, zones, geographies — a department-scoped grudge does not produce this denominator.
  2. Crawl signature: mirror growth (+67,122 files between snapshots) with recursion to depth 15 is crawler behaviour, not manual browsing.
  3. Named collections: 11 surname-style roots (15,385 files) exist, but they read as shadow workspaces, not targeted exfiltration; their mix matches the estate around them.
  4. Dual estate: reaching the bulk file server requires different access than the SharePoint tenancy — two credentials or two compromises, not one aggrieved link-post.
  5. Credential economics: the estate's own audit corpus documents shared and plaintext credentials; a bought or phished credential is a sufficient explanation.

A workforce-positioning test (public RTI salary disclosures, Dec 2025 vs Sep 2026) shows 26,438 posting changes across ~67,000 officers — 39% posting churn in nine months, with several thousand punitive-signature moves (cross-state, urban→rural, pay cut). The test establishes that if the source ever names a person, there is a dataset to check them against; it does not, and cannot, name anyone by itself. Verdict: the insider narrative is unfalsifiable as stated; the structural evidence favours credential compromise harvested by crawl, monetized via the extortion channel. Person-level analysis is maintained in a restricted dossier and intentionally absent from this report.

Strip the incident away and a design property remains — pattern-level, vendor-generic (analysis):

None of this requires zero days. The ToolShell family (CVE-2025-53770 et al.) showed the on-prem tier is a live national target — CISA's July 2025 emergency directive covered exactly this class of server — but this reconstruction needs only a standing read grant and patience.

Chapter 8 — The bank's own record, read from the leak

The corpus contains the bank's own audit and compliance apparatus — an unusual vantage: the institution assessing itself while the assessment sits inside the stolen mirror.

  • Process exists, at scale: TAM/TOM methodology docs, IT-security site inventory (1,488 docs), 259 quarterly user-validation files, VAPT mastersheets with server ownership and remediation triage (Critical/High/Medium/Low).
  • Findings get negotiated: a draft IS-audit report shows management requesting an observation's removal — the lifecycle underperforms the paperwork.
  • Compensating controls are partial: file-integrity monitoring is scoped to mobile-banking servers; the document estate has no equivalent visible control.
  • Hygiene fails at the bottom: one spreadsheet of server details carries plaintext application credentials — counted, never reproduced here.

The picture is not absence of controls; it is controls that stop at the boundary of the tier that actually leaked.

SEBI LODR requires materiality disclosure; the filing chose the narrowest available frame. CERT-In's six-hour rule turns on when the bank knew — a question the public record cannot settle, and which the 12 May listing makes uncomfortable: the group was advertising the estate for ten weeks before the filing.

Chapter 10 — Regulation and the consumer

The incident lands in a regulatory seam. CERT-In's 6-hour directive presumes detection; RBI's cyber framework governs banking systems; SEBI LODR governs disclosure; the DPDP Act's breach regime was not yet in force. The leaked tier — a document estate — belongs clearly to none, which is precisely why the pattern repeats across Indian banking.

For the person whose data is in the corpus, the practical playbook:

  • Assume the mule-KYC bundle means identity fields are circulating: watch for onboarding-in-your-name fraud; pull free credit reports from all four bureaus; dispute unknown accounts.
  • Phishing will now be perfectly targeted — callers will know your account, branch and complaint history. Bank never verifies you by asking for OTPs; hang up and call the printed number.
  • 1930 (cyber fraud helpline) within the golden hour if money moves; complain at cybercrime.gov.in.
  • The bank owes notification under its own fair-practice code and RBI's customer-protection framework — ask for it in writing; the gap between the filing and the corpus is the complaint.

Chapter 11 — Open questions

  • Mirror offline since 24 July 2026; 30+ days of monitoring, no return. Claims about "complete" contents are bounded by three snapshots of a mirror, not the original theft.
  • No mtime: chronology is filename-inferred and biased toward units that date documents.
  • The Digi-Udyam series continuity after 24 June is unobservable; organic pauses can mimic a crawl cutoff.
  • Entry vector: TripleX claims a weak password on 15 Aug 2025; the corpus's dual-estate signature needs at least two grants. Both may be true.
  • The insider narrative: unfalsifiable as stated, but falsifiable the day a name, posting or date is supplied — the test dataset already exists.

Appendix A — Methodology and evidence discipline

Every quantitative claim in this report is registered in a local evidence ledger (246 EVID/CTX entries) with claim text, source artefact (URL or hash-referenced local path), verification status and date. Claims are graded L0–L5 (raw artefact → verified by independent recompute); figures here sit at L2 (distilled from the artefact by us, recomputable) unless marked as claim. Content analysis uses a 91-file high-signal download set processed through a PII-stripping distillation pipeline (1,525 distilled files; 2.3 GB → 5.9 MB). The public inventory is pseudonymized per a redaction manifest; aggregate cells below k=5 are suppressed. Personal leaf data never appears in this report or its published figures.
