The FASTag Bypass, explained simply

For roughly two years, unauthorised software on highway toll booth computers secretly pocketed cash from vehicles without a valid FASTag — printing receipts that looked perfectly honest — at ~100 plazas across 14+ states, while NHAI's national system recorded almost none of it. Police caught it; the audits never did. This page gives you the whole story in five short answers, with the numbers we can actually defend.

1 · In 60 seconds#

What happened. On NH-35 in Mirzapur (UP), the toll contractor's staff ran a second, secret program on the booth computers alongside the official tolling software. When a car without a FASTag came through, the operator collected cash, and the secret program printed a barcoded receipt that looked real. That money never reached the concessionaire or the government. The official system saw a clean, boring day — because the secret program wrote exactly that.

How big. UP's Special Task Force measured ₹45,000/day of suppressed cash at the one plaza it caught (Atraila Shiv Gulam) — about ₹3.3 crore over two years at a single booth. The accused named 42 plazas in 14 states; the ED's forensic examination now points to ~100. Our harm model (full report §13C) prices the plausible total at ₹13–17 crore (conservative) to ₹101–138 crore (aggressive) for the 42 known plazas — and up to several hundred crore if the ~100-plaza footprint ran the same way.

Who. One self-taught developer built and franchised the software. He was arrested near Varanasi airport in January 2025 with laptops, phones and a receipt printer. The collection agencies that ran the plazas employed the booth staff; what the agencies knew is the open question — a court has already found NHAI's first answer to it inadequate.

Why nobody caught it. Audits read what the booth's official software reports. The secret software is the thing reporting. Every "all clear" was the fox filing the henhouse log. The scheme was caught by a police sting, not by any audit, dashboard, or reconciliation flag.

Where it stands. NHAI's February 2025 crackdown (debarments, ~₹100 crore of forfeited guarantees) was set aside by the Delhi High Court in July 2026 — a press note and a confession aren't proof, and no shadow software was found on the petitioners' plazas. In September 2026 the Enforcement Directorate opened a money-laundering case, raided 14 premises, and its device forensics surfaced the ~100-plaza footprint. The careful reading: the fraud is well-evidenced; the map of who knew what is still being drawn.

4 · Questions people actually ask#

Is my FASTag affected? No. Tags, bank accounts and the national clearing system were never compromised. This was cash-lane leakage at the booth — your tag payments flowed normally. The scam's victims were mostly drivers who legally paid cash, and the public purse.

What were "Mobdata" and "Any"? Two names for the shadow software, from ED-attributed reporting. We found no trace of either in app stores, code repositories or certificate logs — consistent with private codenames, not products. (Unrelated to "MobData", a Chinese analytics SDK that shares a name.)

Didn't cameras and audits watch the booths? The audit trail is written by the booth's own computer. A second program on that computer can make any audit look clean. That's the design flaw — and it's generic to every system that delegates its edge to private contractors.

So banning cash fixed it? It removed this particular bypass, and pushed the residual friction elsewhere (and onto those who can't or won't use tags). The court record shows the enforcement response to the underlying fraud was struck down; the software-audit gap remains open. Digital-first is a fine default — but "digital" is not automatically "audited", and pretending otherwise is how a scam runs for two years.

What would real accountability look like? Our RTI asks target the paper trail: how many remittance alerts the exception system generated, plaza by plaza; forensic reports from seized devices; the status of the national toll-reconciliation system. If the state's own numbers exist, publishing them costs nothing.

CashlessConsumer's line. We are pro-digital and pro-consumer — which is why we insist digital systems be transparent, accountable, privacy-respecting, and never compulsory. Liberty to pay cash, and humility about what software can and cannot prove, make payment systems stronger — not weaker.
